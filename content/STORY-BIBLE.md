## 1. Why The Story Is The Product

NexGen has working pricing, working tools, a working live site, a real proof case, and a real owner with fifteen years of receipts. None of that lands without **a story the buyer recognizes themselves inside of.**

Three principles drive every decision in this doc:

### 1.1 Specificity beats abstraction.
"We struggled" is forgettable. "It was a Tuesday in March 2014. The Komax quote came back and I sat in the truck for twenty minutes before I could call my wife." is not. The reader doesn't need *our* struggle — they need to feel *theirs* mirrored by ours. Universal emotion only travels through specific detail.

### 1.2 Vulnerability is credibility.
The shop owner reading this site has been told for years that their problems are uniquely theirs and that the answer is to work harder. The most disarming move we can make is to admit, in print, that we fought the same fight, lost the same nights, made the same wrong bets, and only solved it after we stopped pretending we had it figured out.

### 1.3 The reader has to find themselves on the road, or the road doesn't matter.
Every story beat has to answer: *is this me? am I where he was? am I where he ended up?* If a beat doesn't help the reader place themselves, it's a memoir, not a product page.

## 2. The Story Spine — Five Acts

The arc Brenden actually traveled, mapped to the arc the reader needs to feel.

### Act I — *The Inheritance*
**What it is:** Brenden walks into a shop he didn't design, full of people who'd been doing it the same way for 20 years, in an industry where the "way it's always been done" was the actual liability.
**What the reader feels:** *That's my shop.*
**Beat: ~2014.** Paper schematics. Toolboxes by whoever grabbed them last. Two senior wiremen carrying the entire production capability of the company in their heads. Bid-the-way-Dad-bid-it. Build-the-way-they-always-built-it.

### Act II — *The Tuesday*
**What it is:** The single moment the system broke. Specific day. Specific dollar number. Specific phone call. The night before he couldn't sleep.
**What the reader feels:** *I've had that night.*
**Beat: ~2015-2016.** This is the moment Brenden has to give us in his own words. The lost bid. The walk-out. The customer call that changed the math. The number on the spreadsheet that wasn't a small number.

### Act III — *The Wrong Bets*
**What it is:** The honest catalog of what we tried that didn't work — before what worked, worked.
**What the reader feels:** *I've made those bets too.*
**Beat: 2016-2019.** The first software purchase that sat on the shelf. The first lean consultant who left a binder. The wire processing machine we bought before the workflow was ready. The assumption that more equipment = more output. The painful realization that the bottleneck was never on the floor.

### Act IV — *The Four Pillars*
**What it is:** The system that finally worked, broken into four legs. Each one with its own micro-story.
**What the reader feels:** *That's a system I could actually build.*
**Beats:**
1. **Wire processing automation** — *the machine that doesn't quit at 4:30*
2. **EPLAN** — *the design data IS the production data*
3. **Lean** — *the most expensive thing in any shop is the wireman walking to ask the engineer a question*
4. **Integrated production** — *none of the first three matters if the fourth one isn't holding it together*

### Act V — *The Road You Don't Have To Pave Twice*
**What it is:** The pivot from "look what we did" to "you don't have to do this alone." NexGen as the road map Brenden wishes he'd had.
**What the reader feels:** *I want what he had at the end. I don't want to pay full price for the lessons.*
**Beat: present.** ADS today: 70% faster production, retained senior people, predictable quotes. NexGen as the codified playbook for shops who don't want to spend fifteen years finding it.

## 3. The Deep Interview Kit (Brenden's Homework)

The existing `nexgen-backstory.html` doc has six prompts. Below is the expansion — the actual sub-questions designed to drag specific, usable detail into the open. Brenden records audio answers; Shain transcribes; Chip designs from the transcripts.

> **Format:** Brenden, find a quiet hour. Pick a single prompt at a time. Speak it out loud, recorded on your phone. Don't write — talk. We polish later.

### Prompt 1 — The Inheritance (Act I)
*Talk for ~5 minutes about what you walked into.*

- What year? What was your title? Who was running the shop the day you took over?
- Walk me through the floor as it looked your first week. What was on the wall? What was on the desk? What was on the loading dock?
- Who were the people? Name two specifically — first name only, just so we can write about them as humans. What did they do that no one else could do?
- What did "a good week" look like back then?
- What did "a bad week" look like?
- What were you bidding jobs at? What were you closing them at?

### Prompt 2 — The Tuesday (Act II)
*Tell me about the moment you knew the way you were running things wasn't going to work.*

- What was the day? Approximate is fine — "spring 2015" is enough.
- Was it a customer call? A lost bid? A stockout? An employee leaving? Be specific.
- Walk me from the moment it happened back home that night. What did you eat? What did you say to your wife? Did you sleep?
- What number was on the spreadsheet that scared you? Or what number did you do in your head?
- What did you tell yourself the next morning?

### Prompt 3 — The Wrong Bets (Act III)
*What did you try first that didn't work? Be honest. The reader has tried these too.*

- What's the first piece of software you bought that didn't get adopted? How much did it cost you?
- What's the first piece of equipment you bought that didn't fit the workflow yet?
- What's a consultant or training you paid for that left no trace?
- What's a process change you announced that lasted three weeks?
- What did each one teach you that you didn't see at the time?

### Prompt 4 — The Pillars (Act IV)
*Walk through each of the four pillars one at a time. For each, give me one moment where you saw it work for the first time.*

- **Wire processing.** What machine, what year, what was the first job you ran on it? What was your jaw-on-the-floor moment?
- **EPLAN.** When did you stop fighting it and start using it? Who on your team got it first? What did they unlock?
- **Lean.** What was the first kaizen that actually stuck? Who proposed it? What changed on the floor?
- **Integrated production.** When did the four pillars stop feeling like four projects and start feeling like one operating system? Was there a quarter, a job, a customer that made it click?

### Prompt 5 — The Inflection
*Tell me about the week or month you knew it was working. Not "we made progress" — the moment you knew.*

- What was the throughput, before vs. after?
- Was there a quote you used to lose that you started winning?
- Was there a person you used to need that you didn't anymore?
- Was there a customer who said something that made it real for you? Verbatim if you remember.
- Did you tell anyone? Did you celebrate? What did you feel?

### Prompt 6 — The Why Now (Act V)
*Why are you doing NexGen? Why this, why now, why you?*

- When did shop owners start asking you for the playbook? Name one specifically — first conversation, what they asked.
- What was the moment you decided this had to be a real product, not just favors?
- What do you want NexGen to be for the next person standing where you stood in 2014?
- What's the worst version of NexGen — the version that fails — what does that look like? (This question matters more than it sounds.)
- If you had to put it on a t-shirt, what does NexGen do?

### Bonus prompt — The Sensory Hook
*Just ten things. List them.*

The smell of the shop in summer. The sound the wire feeder makes when it's right. The font on the old work orders. The last thing you'd say to a panel shop owner sitting across from you at lunch. Anything that grounds the story in a body, not a deck.

## 4. The Four Pillars — Micro-Stories

Each pillar gets its own bite-sized story. Same five-act compression: pain → bet → result. Each one ladders to a specific NexGen tier or offering.

### Pillar 1 — Wire Processing Automation
- **Pain:** Wireman + tape measure + wire spool. 4:30 hits and the work stops.
- **Bet:** First Komax / wire processing machine.
- **Result:** Wire is precut, sequenced, labeled. Throughput up; arguments down.
- **Hook line:** *"The machine doesn't quit at 4:30."*
- **Tier ladder:** Panel Shop Automation tier (workflow strategy) → CM Hardware (sensors on the machine).

### Pillar 2 — EPLAN
- **Pain:** Drawings are decoration. Production builds from tribal knowledge that contradicts the drawings.
- **Bet:** EPLAN, with templates and discipline.
- **Result:** Design data IS production data. One source of truth, downstream consequences automatic.
- **Hook line:** *"The drawing is the panel."*
- **Tier ladder:** EPLAN Entry → EPLAN Sprint (project) → Premium Combined.

### Pillar 3 — Lean
- **Pain:** The wireman walks to the engineer's office to ask a question. Multiply by 50 questions per day. That's the most expensive thing in the shop.
- **Bet:** Lean — visual management, standard work, cell-based flow.
- **Result:** Questions get answered before they get asked. Walking goes away. Quality goes up.
- **Hook line:** *"The walking is the waste."*
- **Tier ladder:** Panel Shop Automation → Premium Combined.

### Pillar 4 — Integrated Production
- **Pain:** Three of the four pillars work but the seams between them leak. Engineering builds beautiful drawings the floor can't run. The floor builds in sequence the schedule doesn't expect.
- **Bet:** Tie the pillars into one operating system — workflows, handoffs, dashboards, KPIs.
- **Result:** Throughput jumps a step, then jumps another step. People stop being "the system."
- **Hook line:** *"The fourth pillar is what holds the other three up."*
- **Tier ladder:** Premium Combined → Elite One-on-One.

## 5. Creative Execution — The Concepts

The story is the spine. Below are the breathtaking ways we tell it. Each treatment is an asset class, not a single deliverable. Each one ships across multiple surfaces.

### 5.1 The Evolution Timeline (the centerpiece)
**Format:** Animated, scrubbable, horizontal timeline. Five "stations" mapped to the five acts. As the user scrubs, the shop floor evolves: paper drawings → CAD → EPLAN → integrated dashboard. Equipment evolves alongside. Headcount and throughput numbers tick.
**Tech:** Lottie or hand-built SVG sequence. Embed-ready. ~30 seconds of auto-play, scrubbable manually.
**Voiceover:** Brenden, 30 seconds, low ambient shop sound underneath.
**Surfaces:** Live site backstory section · sales deck slide 3 · LinkedIn screen-recording post · Germany booth loop · cold-email link.

### 5.2 The "Is This You?" Mirror
**Format:** Four-question micro-CYOA inline with the backstory page. Shop snapshot today (manual / partial / automated / integrated). As the reader answers, the timeline highlights the station that matches them. Recommended NexGen tier surfaces alongside the timeline mark.
**Tech:** Same engine as the existing CYOA tools — extend `nexgen-germany-v2.html`.
**Surfaces:** Live site backstory section · standalone landing page for paid-traffic experiments.

### 5.3 The Founder Documentary (3 minutes)
**Format:** Brenden walking the ADS shop floor, mid-shift. Cuts to:
- Old photos (paper schematics on a clipboard, original toolboxes)
- Drone shots of the floor today (organized cells, EPLAN-driven workstations)
- A senior wireman who's been there 20 years, on camera, talking about what changed
- Brenden, on camera, delivering the line: *"I built this so you wouldn't have to break what I broke."*

**Tech:** Real videographer, one shoot day, 5K-10K budget. Cut a 3-min hero, a 60-sec, a 30-sec, six 15-sec clips, three 5-sec stings.
**Surfaces:** Backstory page hero · Germany booth screen · LinkedIn cutdowns · sales follow-up email · ad creative.

### 5.4 The Audio Narrative (Brenden's voice)
**Format:** 8-12 minutes. Brenden reading the final story over low shop ambient sound. Studio-quality audio production. Chapter markers tied to the five acts.
**Tech:** Phone mic is fine for the recording; pro post-production handles the sound. Apple/Spotify/native player on site.
**Surfaces:** Backstory page (embedded player) · podcast pilot episode · Germany booth headphone station · solo cuts as social posts.

### 5.5 Before / After Photographic Set
**Format:** Eight side-by-side panels: same view of the shop, ten years apart. Wire prep area. Engineering desk. Floor traffic patterns. Wall of work orders. Each pair captioned with one specific number ("from 14 wireman walks/day to 2").
**Tech:** Existing photo archive + one shoot day to mirror the angles. Keep it documentary, not stylized.
**Surfaces:** Backstory page (interactive slider) · LinkedIn carousel · trade-show poster series.

### 5.6 The Four Pillars — Animated Build Sequence
**Format:** Each pillar builds itself onto a single dashboard view as the user scrolls. Pillar 1 anchors the screen, Pillar 2 enters and connects, Pillar 3 enters and connects, Pillar 4 envelops them. End state: a single integrated diagram that subtly resembles the live shop floor.
**Tech:** Scroll-driven SVG animation, possibly Three.js if budget allows.
**Surfaces:** Backstory page after the timeline · "How it works" page · sales deck slides 7-10.

### 5.7 The Specific Numbers Wall
**Format:** A stark, design-forward grid of just-the-numbers. White space. Big type. Each number has a footnote-tiny source.
- *70%* — production speed gain
- *11 / 12* — EPLAN customers without ROI before NexGen
- *6,000+* — UL panel shops in the U.S.
- *4* — pillars
- *15* — years of receipts
- *0* — official EPLAN affiliations (it's our credibility, not theirs)
**Tech:** Static page, exquisite typography. Could be a single SVG.
**Surfaces:** Live site (above tier selector) · sales deck (one slide) · printed leave-behind.

### 5.8 The Mirror Quotes — Customer Voice
**Format:** Three to five anonymized quotes from real shop owners describing the moment they realized they needed help. Treated like museum wall plaques — large type, plenty of negative space, attribution as "Panel shop owner, $20M revenue, Midwest."
**Tech:** Editorial design. No fancy tech.
**Surfaces:** Backstory page (between Act II and III) · case studies folder · sales follow-up emails.

### 5.9 The Live Walk-Through (event-only)
**Format:** At Germany and Panel Hackers Summit, Brenden walks visitors through the timeline visualization on a 70" screen, narrating live. 15-min talk track.
**Tech:** Same timeline asset, presenter mode.
**Surfaces:** Trade shows · prospect onsite visits · annual summit.

### 5.10 The Story As A Sales Trigger
**Format:** Within the existing CYOA tools, the story isn't told from page one — it's *unlocked* when the user reveals enough about themselves to deserve it. Reader answers two questions → backstory chapter relevant to their stage appears. The system mirrors them before pitching to them.
**Tech:** Logic extension to existing CYOA tools.
**Surfaces:** All four CYOA tools.

## 10. The Epic Test

Competent storytelling tells the events. Epic storytelling makes the reader *want* to know the storyteller. Most marketing copy lives in the gap between those two.

Below is the test every piece of NexGen story copy gets measured against — the home page, the deck, the documentary script, every LinkedIn post, every email subject line. **A draft that hits fewer than 6 of the 9 isn't ready to ship.**

### The Nine Moves

#### 1. Open in the wound, not the setup
*Don't:* "Fifteen years ago Brenden inherited a panel shop…"
*Do:* "It was a Tuesday — March 11, 2014 — and the bid came back $90,000 under us, and Brenden sat in his truck in the parking lot for forty minutes before he could go home."
The reader gets dropped inside the worst day of someone's life before they know who he is. They keep reading because they have to.

#### 2. Name a villain
A story without an antagonist is a memoir. NexGen's villain is **the lie that hard work, more equipment, and better hiring will eventually be enough**. Whenever you can, point at the lie out loud — the reader is also fighting it. They get to be your ally.

Other named villains in our orbit:
- *Tribal knowledge as a feature instead of a bug.*
- *The training-industrial complex* — software demos that don't change anything, consultants who leave binders.
- *"We've always done it this way."*
- *The myth that adopting EPLAN is the same as using EPLAN.*

#### 3. Specifics that prove you were there
Generic specificity ("a Tuesday he couldn't sleep through") is forgettable. **Real specificity** is a date, a dollar amount, a customer name, a piece of equipment, a temperature, a face. The reader knows you can't make these up.

When in doubt, ask: *would a journalist's fact-checker have something to call?* If the answer is no, your detail isn't specific enough yet.

#### 4. Sensory anchor
The reader's body has to feel something. Truck-cab silence. The smell of solder. The pitch of a wire feeder running right vs. running wrong. The cold blue glow of the EPLAN screen at 11 p.m. when the rest of the shop is dark. Stories travel through bodies; abstractions don't.

#### 5. A line that tattoos itself
Every great story has one line that gets quoted later. Plant it deliberately, then guard it.

Candidates already in the well:
- "*The drawing is the panel.*"
- "*The machine doesn't quit at 4:30.*"
- "*The most expensive thing in any shop is the wireman walking to ask the engineer a question.*"
- "*I built this so you wouldn't have to break what I broke.*"
- "*He already paid. The receipts are on the website.*"
- "*The senior wiremen are still there — and they're craftsmen again, not load-bearing walls.*"

The brand t-shirt line — the *one* — hasn't been written yet. It's hiding in something Brenden will say in the interviews.

#### 6. The turn to the reader
At some point the camera has to swivel. *"Somewhere right now there's another owner sitting in another truck doing math that doesn't work."* The story stops being about Brenden and becomes about the reader. That's when the reader leans in.

This is the move most marketing forgets. Without the turn, you've written an *About Us* page. With the turn, you've written an *About You* page disguised as an About Us page.

#### 7. Honesty about cost
Sanitized stories aren't trusted. The marriage strain. The lost sleep. The bets that almost killed the company. The people who didn't survive the transition. The years lost. **The reader has to feel that the storyteller paid for the privilege of telling them this.**

The cleaner the story, the less the reader believes it.

#### 8. A vow
The story ends with a commitment, not a conclusion. *"He built NexGen so the next owner doesn't have to pay full price for the lessons."* This is the contract — the reader feels that ignoring the offer would mean ignoring a debt.

The vow has to be specific enough to be falsifiable. *"We'll help you scale"* is not a vow. *"You won't waste another quarter on software that doesn't get adopted"* is.

#### 9. Cultural resonance
NexGen is not just a coaching service. It's a small piece of the rebuild of American manufacturing know-how at a moment when the country is finally arguing about that out loud again. Plug into the bigger arc — re-shoring, the trades, working-class dignity, the death of "those who can't, teach" — and your story rides the bigger one without ever explicitly invoking it.

A reader who senses they're being recruited into something larger than a tier subscription buys differently than a reader who thinks they're being sold to.

### What Unlocks Epic

The Epic Test is a measuring stick, not a content generator. The actual molecules — the dates, names, dollars, faces, wifes-told-in-kitchens, lines-spoken-at-lunch — only Brenden has. Everything Shain or Claude writes before Brenden's interviews is *placeholder shape*. Useful as a mold; not the casting.

The seven unlock questions (asked of Brenden, voice-memo length, no editing required):

1. **What was the actual Tuesday?** Date, customer, dollar, lost-by-how-much.
2. **Where were you when you learned?** Sensory anchor.
3. **What did you do in the next 40 minutes? The next night?** Pacing — the waiting is where the reader connects.
4. **Who were the two senior wiremen?** First names, character, are they still there?
5. **What was the first wrong bet?** Software / equipment / consultant — cost and year.
6. **What's the lie you most want to call out, in your own words?** The villain.
7. **If you could stop one panel-shop owner from making the same mistake, what would you tell him at lunch?** The brand t-shirt line is hiding in here.

These are 30 minutes of voice memo. Drop the file in `content/raw/audio-epic-unlocks.m4a` and everything downstream — the home page rewrite, the documentary script, the deck overhaul, the EPLAN partnership memo, the ad creative — gets a level of specificity competent storytelling can never reach.

> *The story is sitting inside Brenden, intact. Our job is to extract it without sanding off the parts that hurt to remember. Those are the parts the reader trusts.*
