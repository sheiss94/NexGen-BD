## Draft (ready to paste into LinkedIn)

In the spring of 2014, I walked into a panel building hall in Nuremberg.

Software, automation, workflow, manufacturing — operating as one connected system. No one fighting through a pile of prints to push a job out the door.

The future already existed. Most of us in the United States just hadn't seen it yet.

I came home convinced the answer was machines. Bought our first Steinhauer ModCenter CNC. Precision arrived almost overnight.

I thought I'd found the lever.

I hadn't.

For the next two-and-a-half years, our quality went up and our throughput went up — and the bids kept coming back ten to twenty percent under. Close enough to keep bidding. Far enough that profitability slipped a little further every quarter.

I spent the first year convinced our competitors were cutting corners.

They weren't. They were better.

Better quality. Faster builds. Lower costs. Their margins were holding while their prices were dropping. They had figured out something I hadn't, and the longer it took me to admit it, the further behind I got.

I tried what every sensible owner tries. More automation. Consultants. Higher-skilled engineers. Three salespeople in two years. Longer hours. Heavier reliance on the senior wiremen who carried our entire production capability in their heads.

None of it moved the needle.

Then the real lesson landed:

**Machines alone aren't enough.**

Software without process creates struggle. Automation without standards creates chaos. The hardest part of modernization was never buying the technology. It was rebuilding the workflow.

What finally moved it was an order of operations.

Lean first.
Standards second.
Cell-based production.
Employee engagement.
Continuous improvement.

THEN EPLAN started to land — because the systems could absorb it.
THEN automation paid off — because the workflows could feed it.

Eventually those initiatives stopped being separate things. They started working together as one connected system — the same thing I'd seen in Nuremberg, but built for an American panel shop, in the shop I'd built.

The goal was never to remove craftsmanship.

The goal was to remove struggle.

Today our production runs about 70% faster than it did. Not because the people work harder — because the system works better.

Good people who used to be trapped inside inefficient systems are free to focus on craftsmanship and quality again.

Every few months now, another shop owner shows up asking the same questions I once asked and didn't get answers to:

› How do we make EPLAN work in the real world?
› How do we move from tribal knowledge to scalable systems?
› How do we improve without overwhelming our team?
› How do we modernize without breaking everything?

NexGen Panel Mentoring is the answer that doesn't fit in a coffee meeting.

I built it so the next owner — currently bidding ten percent over the shop down the road, working longer hours, hiring the wrong consultants — doesn't have to take three years to figure out what we already paid for.

I already paid. The receipts are on the website.

## Rhythm notes for Brenden

- **First three lines are everything on LinkedIn.** The "Spring 2014, Nuremberg" hook reads as a true story, not a marketing post. Don't change it unless something stronger surfaces.
- **The double-line breaks are deliberate.** LinkedIn collapses single line breaks into paragraphs but preserves doubles. The white space is what makes long posts read fast.
- **Bullets:** the four owner questions land harder as a stacked block than as prose. Keep them stacked.
- **First-person:** the post is written in Brenden's voice. *"I walked,"* *"I came home,"* *"I built it."* If you adapt, keep the I.
- **Don't add hashtags at the end.** This story stands without #manufacturing #lean #electricalengineering — those signal "marketing post." This post is a *founder telling his story*.
- **Image to attach:** ideal — a photo of the shop floor today, or a photo from the Nuremberg trip, or the Steinhauer ModCenter. Real photos > stock. If nothing's available, the story is strong enough alone.
- **First comment after posting:** drop the panelmentor.com link in the first comment so the post itself stays uncluttered.
