The Premise

Most company stories tell the events. Epic stories make you want to know the storyteller.

The first version of NexGen's story sounded like a competent paragraph in a brochure — and "competent" is the worst thing a story can be. Here's what we're aiming for instead. The blue specifics are placeholders — Brenden has the real ones.

It was a Tuesday — March 11, 2014 — and the Carmichael bid came back from a shop in Wisconsin ninety thousand dollars under us, and Brenden Fritz sat in his truck in the parking lot for forty minutes before he could go home and tell his wife. The shop he'd inherited ran on paper, on coffee, on the memory of two senior wiremen who could quit on a Tuesday and end the company by Friday. The drawings were decoration. The schedule was a wish. And underneath it all was the lie every shop owner in America has been told for thirty years — that working harder, hiring smarter, and buying more equipment would eventually be enough. It isn't. It never was. What finally broke the lie wasn't a machine. It was a system: wire processing automation so the work didn't stop at 4:30, EPLAN so the drawing finally was the panel, Lean so a wireman didn't walk forty feet to ask the engineer a question that should have been answered the day before, and integrated production to hold the other three together like rebar in concrete. ADS runs 70% faster now. The senior wiremen are still there — and they're craftsmen again, not load-bearing walls. NexGen Panel Mentoring exists because the gap was never the software, it was adoption — and because somewhere right now there's another owner sitting in another truck doing math that doesn't work. Brenden built NexGen so the next one doesn't have to pay full price for the lessons. He already paid. The receipts are on the website.

The Carmichael bid is fabricated. So is March 11. So is ninety thousand. So is forty minutes. We're holding open seats — Brenden fills them with the real moments, dates, dollars, and names. Real specifics are unforgeable. Fabricated ones get smelled out by the third read.

The Spine · Five Acts

"Ninety thousand dollars under."

The Wrong Bets

The honest catalog of what didn't work — software ahead of workflow, equipment ahead of standards, consultants who left binders.

"The bottleneck was never on the floor."

Wire processing automation. EPLAN. Lean. Integrated production. Four legs. One operating system.

"The drawing is the panel."

The Epic Test

Nine moves that separate epic from competent.

Every NexGen draft — site copy, deck slide, social post, ad — gets measured against this. 6 of 9 to ship. 8 of 9 for the home page.

01

Open in the wound

Drop the reader inside the worst day before they know whose day it is. Don't set up the story.

02

Name a villain

The lie that hard work + more equipment = enough. Tribal knowledge as a feature. The training-industrial complex.

03

Specifics a journalist could fact-check

Date. Customer. Dollar. Equipment model. Year. Generic isn't specific.

04

Sensory anchor

Truck-cab silence. The smell of solder. The pitch of a wire feeder running right. Bodies remember bodies.

05

A line that tattoos itself

One quotable line per piece. Plant it on purpose. Guard it.

06

The turn to the reader

The camera swivels. The story stops being about Brenden and becomes about whoever is reading.

07

Honest about cost

The marriage strain. The lost sleep. The bets that almost killed it. Sanitized = unbelievable.

08

A specific, falsifiable vow

"You won't waste another quarter on software that doesn't get adopted." Not "we'll help you scale."

09

Cultural resonance

NexGen is a small piece of the rebuild of American manufacturing know-how. Plug into the bigger arc.

For Brenden

Thirty minutes. Seven questions.

Voice memo. While you're driving home. No editing, no polish, no script. The story is sitting inside you intact — our job is to extract it without sanding off the parts that hurt to remember. Those are the parts the reader trusts.

  1. What was the actual Tuesday? The day. The customer. The dollar amount. Lost-by-how-much.Anchors the entire story to one real event.
  2. Where were you when you learned? Truck? Kitchen? Phone?The sensory anchor. Bodies remember bodies.
  3. What did you do in the next forty minutes? The next night?The waiting is where the reader connects.
  4. Who were the two senior wiremen? First names. What were they like? Are they still there?If they're still there, that's the payoff line. If they left, that's a different story — also true.
  5. What was the first wrong bet — software, equipment, consultant? Cost? Year?Vulnerability. The reader has made the same bets.
  6. What's the lie you most want to call out, in your own words?Names the villain. Without one, the story is a memoir.
  7. If you could stop one panel-shop owner from making your same mistake, what would you tell him at lunch?The brand t-shirt line is hiding in here.
