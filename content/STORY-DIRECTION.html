The Premise

Most company stories tell the events. Epic stories make you want to know the storyteller.

A first attempt at this story tried to compress the turning point into a single dramatic Tuesday. That's wrong — businesses don't break in one Tuesday. They erode in thirty quarters. v0.2 trades cinematic drama for emotional precision — the truer shape, the more recognizable shape. Italic blue specifics below are placeholders. Brenden has the real ones.

There wasn't a Tuesday. There wasn't a single phone call. There wasn't a spreadsheet number that finally made the math stop working. There were two-and-a-half years of losing bids — not by sixty percent, not by half — by ten to twenty percent. Close enough to keep bidding. Far enough that profitability slipped a little further every quarter, and then a little further the next one.

Brenden Fritz spent the first year convinced his competitors were cutting corners. They weren't. Their quality was better than his. Their build times were shorter. Their costs were lower. Their margins were holding while their prices were dropping. They had figured out something he hadn't, and the longer it took him to admit it, the further behind he got.

He tried everything a sensible owner tries. More automated equipment. Consultants who left binders. Higher-skilled engineers. Three salespeople in two years — none of them lasted, because there was nothing competitive to sell. He worked longer hours and leaned harder on the two senior wiremen who carried the entire production capability of the company in their heads. None of it moved the needle by a single point.

What finally moved it wasn't a purchase. It was an order of operations. Lean first, to see the waste. Systems second, to codify what worked. Culture third, to get the floor to own it. Commitment fourth, to refuse to half-ass any of it. Only then did EPLAN start to land — because by then the systems could absorb it. Only then did automation pay off — because by then the workflows could feed it. The shops that had been winning his bids for years had built that sequence patiently. Brenden had been trying to start at step five.

Today ADS runs seventy percent faster than it did. The senior wiremen are still here — and they're craftsmen again, not load-bearing walls. The salespeople have something competitive to sell. Margins are healthier than they've been in a decade. And every few months, another shop owner shows up asking the same question Brenden once asked and didn't get an answer to: "How did you do this?"

NexGen Panel Mentoring is the answer that won't fit in a coffee meeting. It's the order of operations Brenden built by reverse-engineering his own loss column — so the next owner currently bidding ten percent over the shop down the road, working longer hours, hiring the wrong consultants, doesn't have to take three years to figure out what we already paid for.

He already paid. The receipts are on the website.

The blue specifics — 2.5 years, three salespeople, 70% — are placeholders. Brenden replaces them with the real numbers in his seven-question voice memo. The order-of-operations insight (Lean → Systems → Culture → Commitment → THEN EPLAN → THEN automation) is the new core thesis: most shops fail not because they pick the wrong tools, but because they install them before the foundation exists.

The Epic Test

Nine moves that separate epic from competent.

Every NexGen draft — site copy, deck slide, social post, ad — gets measured against this. 6 of 9 to ship. 8 of 9 for the home page.

01

Open in the wound

Drop the reader inside the worst day before they know whose day it is. Don't set up the story.

02

Name a villain

The lie that hard work + more equipment = enough. Tribal knowledge as a feature. The training-industrial complex.

03

Specifics a journalist could fact-check

Date. Customer. Dollar. Equipment model. Year. Generic isn't specific.

04

Sensory anchor

Truck-cab silence. The smell of solder. The pitch of a wire feeder running right. Bodies remember bodies.

05

A line that tattoos itself

One quotable line per piece. Plant it on purpose. Guard it.

06

The turn to the reader

The camera swivels. The story stops being about Brenden and becomes about whoever is reading.

07

Honest about cost

The marriage strain. The lost sleep. The bets that almost killed it. Sanitized = unbelievable.

08

A specific, falsifiable vow

"You won't waste another quarter on software that doesn't get adopted." Not "we'll help you scale."

09

Cultural resonance

NexGen is a small piece of the rebuild of American manufacturing know-how. Plug into the bigger arc.

For Brenden

Thirty minutes. Seven questions.

Voice memo. While you're driving home. No editing, no polish, no script. The story is sitting inside you intact — our job is to extract it without sanding off the parts that hurt to remember. Those are the parts the reader trusts.

  1. The first quarter you knew something was wrong — what was it? What was the win/loss column doing? What were you telling yourself about why?Anchors the gradient. Establishes the unease before the realization.
  2. The competitor or competitors you watched pull ahead. What were they doing differently? When did you stop assuming they were cutting corners and start assuming they were just better?Names the antagonist as competence, not villainy. That's harder and more interesting.
  3. What did you try FIRST that didn't work? Walk me through the sequence — equipment, consultants, hires, salespeople. Costs. Years. What you thought you were buying vs. what you got.Vulnerability. The reader has made every one of these bets too.
  4. The two senior wiremen. First names. What did they each do? What would have happened if either had quit during the worst stretch? Are they still here today — and what role do they play now?Sets up the most powerful payoff in the story. Or the most honest if it ends differently.
  5. The breakthrough — when did you stop trying things and start building the system? Was there a book, a person, a kaizen, a moment? What was the first principle that finally clicked?The hinge of the entire story. Without this, there's no Act III.
  6. Your version of the order of operations. What came first, second, third in the turnaround? When did EPLAN finally land? When did automation finally pay off? Why didn't they pay off earlier when you tried them?This IS the brand insight. The reader has to feel why the order matters.
  7. The first time another shop owner asked you "how did you do this?" Who, when, what did they ask, what did you tell them? Did they actually use what you told them — or did they make the same wrong bets you'd already made?The brand t-shirt line is hiding in here. So is the genesis of NexGen.
