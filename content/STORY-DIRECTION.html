The Premise

Most company stories tell the events. Epic stories make you want to know the storyteller.

v0.3 weaves Brenden's authentic narrative — the 2014 SPS Drives trip, the Steinhauer ModCenter CNC purchase, his realization that "machines alone aren't enough" — into the gradient framing v0.2 introduced. The result is the truest version we've written — anchored in a moment that actually happened, ending on the four questions Brenden's narrative names verbatim. Italic blue specifics are still placeholders that get refined.

In the spring of 2014, Brenden Fritz walked into the panel building hall at SPS Drives in Nuremberg, and saw — for the first time — what panel manufacturing could become. Software, automation, workflow, and manufacturing operating as one connected system. No one fighting through a pile of prints to push a job out the door. The future of the industry was already in that room. Most of us in the United States just hadn't seen it yet.

He came home convinced the answer was machines. ADS bought its first Steinhauer ModCenter CNC, and the precision and consistency arrived almost overnight. He thought he'd found the lever.

He hadn't. Not the whole one.

Over the next two-and-a-half years, the quality got better and the throughput went up — and the bids kept coming back ten to twenty percent under. Close enough to keep bidding. Far enough that profitability slipped a little further every quarter, and a little further the next one. His competitors weren't cutting corners. Their quality was better than his. Their build times were shorter. Their costs were lower. Their margins were holding while their prices were dropping. They had figured out something he hadn't, and the longer it took him to admit it, the further behind he got.

He tried everything a sensible owner tries. More automated equipment. Consultants who left binders. Higher-skilled engineers. Three salespeople in two years — none of them lasted, because there was nothing competitive to sell. Longer hours. Heavier reliance on the senior wiremen who carried the entire production capability of the company in their heads. None of it moved the needle by a single point.

That's when the real lesson landed. Machines alone aren't enough. Software without process creates struggle. Automation without standards creates chaos. The hardest part of modernization was never buying the technology — it was rebuilding the workflow, training the people, standardizing the process, and staying committed long enough to reach the payoff.

What finally moved it was an order of operations. ADS rebuilt around Lean manufacturing, standardization, cell-based production, employee engagement, and continuous improvement. Then EPLAN started to land — because by then the systems could absorb it. Then automation paid off — because by then the workflows could feed it. Eventually those initiatives stopped being separate things and started working together as one connected system — the same thing Brenden had seen in Nuremberg, but built for an American panel shop, by Americans, in the shop he'd built.

The goal was never to remove craftsmanship. The goal was to remove struggle.

Today ADS runs about seventy percent faster than it did. Not because the people work harder — because the system works better. Engineering flows into manufacturing. Information moves with clarity. Good people who used to be trapped inside inefficient systems are free to focus on quality, craftsmanship, and continuous improvement again.

And every few months now, another owner shows up asking the same four questions Brenden once asked and didn't get answers to:
How do we make EPLAN work in the real world?
How do we move from tribal knowledge to scalable systems?
How do we improve without overwhelming our team?
How do we modernize without breaking everything?
NexGen Panel Mentoring is the answer that won't fit in a coffee meeting. It's the order of operations Brenden built by walking the harder version of the road first — so the next owner currently bidding ten percent over the shop down the road, working longer hours, hiring the wrong consultants, doesn't have to take three years to figure out what we already paid for.

He already paid. The receipts are on the website.

The Epic Test

Nine moves that separate epic from competent.

Every NexGen draft — site copy, deck slide, social post, ad — gets measured against this. 6 of 9 to ship. 8 of 9 for the home page.

01

Open in the wound

Drop the reader inside the worst day before they know whose day it is. Don't set up the story.

02

Name a villain

The lie that hard work + more equipment = enough. Tribal knowledge as a feature. The training-industrial complex.

03

Specifics a journalist could fact-check

Date. Customer. Dollar. Equipment model. Year. Generic isn't specific.

04

Sensory anchor

Truck-cab silence. The smell of solder. The pitch of a wire feeder running right. Bodies remember bodies.

05

A line that tattoos itself

One quotable line per piece. Plant it on purpose. Guard it.

06

The turn to the reader

The camera swivels. The story stops being about Brenden and becomes about whoever is reading.

07

Honest about cost

The marriage strain. The lost sleep. The bets that almost killed it. Sanitized = unbelievable.

08

A specific, falsifiable vow

"You won't waste another quarter on software that doesn't get adopted." Not "we'll help you scale."

09

Cultural resonance

NexGen is a small piece of the rebuild of American manufacturing know-how. Plug into the bigger arc.

For Brenden

Thirty minutes. Seven questions.

Voice memo. While you're driving home. No editing, no polish, no script. The story is sitting inside you intact — our job is to extract it without sanding off the parts that hurt to remember. Those are the parts the reader trusts.

  1. Nuremberg, 2014 — paint the moment. What month? Who did you go with? What specifically did you see in that panel building hall that broke your frame? Was it a single demo or the cumulative effect?This is the inciting incident. The more sensory detail you can give, the better the opening lands.
  2. The Steinhauer ModCenter CNC — the buy. How long after Nuremberg? What other machines did you consider? What did you tell the leadership team you expected? What was the first job that ran through it, and what did the floor say after that first run?First wrong answer. Reader has bought their version of this.
  3. The first year after the CNC — when did you start realizing it wasn't enough on its own? What was the win/loss column doing? What competitors were you watching pull ahead, and what were they doing differently?Anchors the gradient and names the antagonist as competence, not villainy.
  4. What did you try AFTER the CNC that didn't work? Walk me through the sequence — more automation, consultants, engineers, salespeople. Costs. Years. What each one was supposed to fix.Vulnerability. The reader has made every one of these same bets.
  5. The moment "machines alone aren't enough" landed. What broke through? Was it a book, a person, a quote, a failed initiative? Where were you when you said the words out loud?The hinge of the entire story. Without this, there's no Act IV.
  6. The order of operations as you actually built it. Lean, standardization, cell-based, employee engagement, continuous improvement — what came first, what was hardest, what surprised you? When did EPLAN finally land for real? When did automation finally pay off? Why didn't they pay off earlier?THIS is the brand insight. The reader has to feel why the order matters.
  7. The first time another shop owner asked you "how did you do this?" Who, when, what did they ask, what did you tell them? Did they go off and use it — or make the same wrong bets you'd already made?The genesis of NexGen. The brand t-shirt line is hiding in here.
